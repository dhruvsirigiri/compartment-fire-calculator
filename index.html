Step 1

How big is the fire?

The heat release rate (HRR) is how much energy a fire gives off each second, in kilowatts (kW). Smoke temperature, smoke production and flashover all depend on it, so every later step uses this curve as its input.

Q(t) = α · t2

Q is the heat release rate in kW, t is time since ignition in seconds, and α is the growth coefficient in kW/s². Because time is squared, doubling the time makes the fire four times bigger. The fire then holds at a peak HRR set by what is burning.

Growth ratetg (s)α (kW/s²)Typical fuel

Each rate is defined by the time tg to reach 1,055 kW (1,000 BTU/s), so α = 1055 / tg².

Step 2

How hot does the smoke get?

The plume carries hot gas to the ceiling, where it forms a smoke layer. The MQH method (McCaffrey, Quintiere and Harkleroad, 1981) predicts the layer's average temperature from an energy balance: heat comes in from the fire and leaves out the door and into the walls.

ΔTg = 6.85 · [ Q2 / (Ao√Ho · hk · AT) ]1/3
  • Ao√Ho, the opening factor: door area times the square root of its height. Taller doors vent hot gas better.
  • AT, the inside surface area of walls, ceiling and floor minus the door.
  • hk, how easily the walls absorb heat. Early on, heat only soaks into the surface: hk = √(kρc/t). After the thermal penetration time tp = (ρc/k)(δ/2)², heat flows through the wall: hk = k/δ.

Step 3

When does the room flash over?

Flashover is the sudden switch from a fire burning one object to a room where every exposed surface is burning. Radiation from the hot smoke layer heats everything below it until it all ignites at once. After flashover, nobody in the room survives, and the fire can spread to the rest of the building. Three classic methods estimate the smallest fire that can cause it:

Babrauskas: QFO = 750 · Ao√Ho
Thomas: QFO = 7.8 · AT + 378 · Ao√Ho
MQH: QFO = 610 · (hk · AT · Ao√Ho)1/2

The MQH version is just Step 2 solved backwards for the fire size that makes the smoke about 500 °C hotter than ambient, so the calculator reads it straight off the temperature curve. The methods disagree, which is normal. Engineers look at the whole range rather than trusting one number.

The air supply limit. A fire needs oxygen, and in a closed room almost all of it comes through the door. The air that can flow in is about 0.5 · Ao√Ho kg/s, and burning 1 kg of air releases about 3,000 kJ. So the biggest fire the door can support is roughly Qvent = 1,500 · Ao√Ho kW. The calculator caps the fire at this value. A fire held back by its air supply is called ventilation-controlled.

Step 4

Do people get out in time?

Fire safety design compares two times. ASET (available safe egress time) is how long until the room becomes untenable. RSET (required safe egress time) is how long people need to get out. The design works only if ASET is longer than RSET.

Smoke filling. The plume pulls in, or entrains, room air as it rises, so it carries much more mass to the ceiling than the fire itself produces. The calculator uses Heskestad's plume equation, with 70% of the fire's heat carried by the plume:

ṁp = 0.071 · Qc1/3 · z5/3 + 0.0018 · Qc  (kg/s)

z is the height of the smoke layer's bottom edge. Each second, the calculator adds the plume's mass to the layer, subtracts the smoke flowing out of the top of the door once the layer drops below the door top, and converts the layer's mass to a depth using the smoke density from Step 2. That is a small differential equation solved step by step, the same idea as Euler's method in calculus.

Untenable here means the earlier of two events: the smoke layer drops to 1.8 m (head height, so people breathe it), or the layer reaches 200 °C (painful radiant heat from above). RSET is detection time plus reaction time plus walking time.

Limits

What this calculator leaves out

  • One room with one opening. Smoke spreading to other rooms is not modeled.
  • The fire sits on the floor and never decays or burns out.
  • No sprinklers. A sprinkler would cap or shrink the fire once it activates.
  • The smoke layer uses one average temperature (from MQH), and MQH is a fit to tests below about 600 °C.
  • Untenability is judged by layer height and temperature only, not by toxic gases or visibility.
  • The occupant walks straight to the door at a steady speed.

Real designs use full zone models such as CFAST, or CFD models such as FDS, both free from NIST, along with safety factors. This tool shows the physics those models are built on.
